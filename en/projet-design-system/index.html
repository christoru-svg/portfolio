Case study · 2026

Overhauling Oodrive's design system without freezing the product roadmap

How I turned a shared observation (scattered libraries and a lot of custom CSS) into a phased overhaul plan, presented to and decided on with our CPO.

Client
Oodrive (internal)
Role
Design system lead designer
Duration
Q4 2025 → Q4 2026
Status
In progress
01 · Context

Libraries that existed, but no system

Oodrive wasn't starting from scratch: on the design side, one Figma library per platform (Web, iOS, Android) plus an icon and illustration library; on the tech side, a front-end component library. But each one lived its own life, with no shared foundations and no contribution rules.

In Q4 2025, I led an audit of what existed (interfaces and code) to identify critical components and choose the structure and platform for the future design system.

Key takeaway: the challenge wasn't designing components, it was getting buy-in that a design system is a product investment, with a capacity cost and a measurable return.

02 · Problem

UI debt slowing down every feature

The audit confirmed what teams felt day to day: without a shared system, every new feature reinvents part of its interface.

  • UI inconsistencies from one product and platform to another
  • Frequent back-and-forth between design and development on features
  • A lot of custom CSS: more than two months of delay on a signature feature in summer 2025
  • Difficulty planning ahead for a future desktop app
  • More costly collaboration with external vendors

“Systems solve the easy problems so Products can solve hard problems more easily.”

Nathan Curtis, the closing quote of the pitch
03 · Solution

A single source of truth, from Figma to Storybook

Rather than yet another library, I proposed a single foundation, synced between the design library (Figma) and the tech library (Storybook), with a clear goal for the first half of 2026: ship a solid design system foundation. The expected benefits: 20 to 30% faster time-to-market, more consistency, scalability and more efficient teams.

  • Six categories: overview, foundations, guidelines, UI components, page templates and layouts, governance
  • UI components with a flattened structure, to make them easier to use and keep the system scalable
  • Tokenized foundations: colors (palette and roles), typography, spacing, radius, shadows, elevations, grid, iconography
04 · Approach

A phased approach, without a freeze

Freezing features while rebuilding the interface wasn't an option. So each phase comes with a simple rule that frames ongoing work rather than stopping it.

Phase 1 · Q4 2025

Audit and prioritization

Understand what exists, identify critical components, choose the structure and platform. Rule: every new feature reuses the existing components identified.

Phase 2 · Foundations

Tokens and shared basics

Colors, typography, spacing, radius and themes. Rule: no new structural UI feature until the foundations are stable.

Phase 3 · Two parallel streams

Components on one side, rules and contribution on the other

Real feature needs feed the components, while documentation, versioning and the contribution process take shape. Rule: every UI change is documented and versioned.

Phase 4 · Q4 2026

Measurement and improvement

KPIs, team feedback and continuous adjustments: design system priorities are driven by data and feedback from the field.

Leadership

Giving product leadership real decision levers

Rather than asking for “resources,” I presented the CPO with three costed scenarios, each with its capacity, expected results by the end of June, what would need to be deprioritized and its risks: distributed contribution across several designers, developers and UX writers; a dedicated pair of owners, one designer and one developer; or intermittent capacity.

The CPO approved the first scenario, distributed contribution: design system adoption on half of new features on the design side, the foundations and the first three components, UX writing and accessibility guidelines, and contribution rules, in exchange for freezing non-critical initiatives.

Other priorities then pulled the team away, and the project switched to the second scenario, the dedicated pair of owners. Because every scenario had been costed up front, this fallback happened without renegotiating the project: we already knew what we were keeping (the foundations, a base for what comes next) and what we were willing to lose (slower adoption, fewer components in the short term).

05 · Results

Where the project stands

Even with reduced capacity, the project moved forward: the foundations are available in Storybook and a first component has shipped. We're now tackling the priority components, alongside major roadmap initiatives. That's exactly the “without a freeze” principle set out in the pitch: the design system grows with the product, not instead of it.

What I'd do differently

The ideal scenario relied on several part-time contributors, the resource most exposed to shifting priorities. Today, I would present the dedicated pair of owners as the backbone of the project, and distributed contribution as an accelerator to switch on when the roadmap allows.
