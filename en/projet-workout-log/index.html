Workout Log a gym log that listens to your back

How I designed and shipped, in one week, a strength-training app adapted to my herniated disc, built around one simple insight: discomfort doesn't show up during the exercise, but the next day.

01 · Context

A physiotherapy program, and apps built for performance

I have an L5‑S1 herniated disc. My physiotherapist gave me a precise program: two sessions a week, sets of 15 at moderate load, core work for the transverse and obliques, and back extensions on a 45° bench.

The training apps I was using are built for performance. They highlight total volume and personal records, and offer nothing to track pain or follow a medical instruction.

Key takeaway: the user was me. The safeguard was my physiotherapist: the app never presents an exercise as "safe" on its own, every status comes from her.

02 · Problem

Discomfort shows up the next day, and no app asks about it

Using those apps, I noticed my discomfort almost never appeared during the exercise. It came the next morning, when I woke up. An app that only measures the workout misses the one piece of information that matters for my back.

  • The physiotherapist's instructions stay on a sheet of paper, far from the moment of effort
  • Highlighting total volume pushes you to load up, the opposite of the goal
  • No link between a workout, its exercises and the pain of the following days

"Progress without paying for it the next day: only increase the load if my back stays calm in the days that follow."

Project goal
03 · Identity

Calm, clean, readable from a meter away

Before writing any code, I set out the art direction, four key screens and five user flows in a proposal page. Three choices shape the identity.

  • Traffic-light colors reserved for the physio status: green, amber and red are never decorative. When you see them, they are about your back.
  • Atkinson Hyperlegible: a typeface designed for low vision, so it stays readable at the gym, sweaty, with the phone lying on a bench.
  • A logo that says what the app is: a first spine-shaped idea read like a gauge. The final version pairs a dumbbell with two notebook lines.
04 · Solution

Two check-ins: during the exercise, and the next morning

I analysed the training app I was using. I kept its workout cards and the accordion overview during the session. I adapted the rest: consistency and pain come before total volume, and every exercise shows its full format and its physio instruction.

  • During the workout: one big "Log set" button, the rest time between each set, "Last time: 30 kg × 15", and a "It pulls" button to flag discomfort on a set.
  • The next morning: a 30-second check-in. Morning pain compared with before and right after the workout, stiffness, leg symptoms, and what else may have played a part outside the gym.
  • Progression tied to the next days: the app only suggests +2.5 kg after two easy sessions with next-day pain below the threshold set with the physiotherapist.
  • Leads, not diagnoses: when one workout leads to more painful mornings, the app lists the exercises specific to it, to show the physiotherapist.

The app interface is in French.

05 · Method

Ship fast, audit, then iterate after every workout

Rather than static mockups, I prototyped directly in real code, with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code). I made every design decision and approved every iteration; the AI let me test my choices at the gym within the first week.

Phase 1 · Framing

Art direction and user flows proposal

Art direction, four key screens, five user flows (setup, adding an exercise, creating a workout, workout in progress, summary for the physiotherapist) and a data model.

Phase 2 · MVP

An installable, offline-first PWA

Installable without an app store and usable without signal in the gym basement. Tested flow by flow before each release.

Phase 3 · Audit

Nielsen and technical audit, priorities fixed

An evaluation against Nielsen's 10 heuristics, with a technical part. Six priorities fixed: a double tap that logged two sets, no way to add an exercise mid-workout, no onboarding, screen-reader accessibility…

Phase 4 · Usage iterations

Every real workout brings feedback

Gestures (long press, swipe to delete with undo), free workout, side-by-side plank timer, estimated duration, pain logging at any time, discomfort alerts. Some ideas were also removed, like offering all 16 illustrations for every new exercise.

The incident

Losing my data, and making safety a first-class feature

Clearing my browser data wiped all my workouts. The export reminder I had planned wasn't enough. I added automatic cloud sync (Google sign-in, Firebase) and a shareable backup copy to Drive.

For a health tracking app, data safety isn't a settings option: it's a promise made to the user from the very first screen.

What I'd do differently

I would handle data backup from the very first version, not after an incident. I would also have other people in lower-back rehabilitation test the app earlier: being your own user speeds everything up, but forces you to separate what you feel from what generalises.
